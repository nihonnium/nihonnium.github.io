Bi-Weekly Production Diary

This week, I focused on learning the connection between biological intelligence and machine learning. I have been analyzing the paper "Biological Underpinnings for Lifelong Learning Machines" to better understand how neural plasticity can be modeled in AI.

I am most happy with how this research directly feeds into my internship. Understanding how biological systems protect old memories while acquiring new ones provides a solid theoretical foundation for my work on "catastrophic forgetting" in robotics.

I found it difficult to grasp some of the more complex neurobiological mechanisms, such as synaptic scaling and neuromodulation, and how they can be efficiently translated into computational algorithms without immense overhead.

From this work, I learned that lifelong learning is not just about data retention, but about dynamic stability. I learned that mechanisms like "synaptic consolidation" are key to building more robust autonomous systems that don't reset their knowledge when exposed to new environments.

Next time I want to improve by implementing a small Python simulation of a simple EWC (Elastic Weight Consolidation) algorithm, which is one of the practical applications of these biological theories in machine learning.

In the last two weeks, I officially started my role as a Research Intern under Dr. Pencheng Liu. I attended my first reading group meeting and gave a presentation on the research papers I have been studying.

I am most happy with my presentation performance. Sharing my findings with the group helped me better understand how biological learning connects to artificial intelligence.

I found it difficult to fully grasp how complex brain mechanisms, like neuromodulation, can be turned into computer code without making the system too slow or heavy.

From this work, I learned a lot from the paper "Preserving and combining knowledge in robotic lifelong reinforcement learning." It described an experiment with a robotic arm that learns new skills without forgetting old ones. This is a great example of how to solve catastrophic forgetting in real-world robotics.

Next time I want to improve by looking for ways to apply these theories to search and rescue robots. While I continue reading more papers, I will focus on how robots can learn and adapt in emergency situations without losing their previous training.

In the last two weeks, I used the Easter break to focus more on reading research papers. I also visited the Institute for Safe Autonomy labs at the University of York, where I saw many inspiring autonomous projects. Finally, I participated in a workshop with Dr. Pencheng Liu’s team and Prof. Heedae Kim’s research group from Jeonbuk National University (South Korea).

I am most happy with presenting my research findings to the international group. It was a great experience to answer their questions and learn about the different projects being conducted by the South Korean team and my own colleagues.

I found it difficult to explain complex ideas clearly during the Q&A session of the workshop, but it helped me identify which parts of my research need more detail.

From this work, I learned that seeing real-world robots at the Institute for Safe Autonomy makes the theory much clearer. It helped me understand how "safety" is just as important as "learning" when building autonomous systems.

Next time I want to improve by using the inspiration from the lab visit to refine my ideas for search and rescue robots. I plan to focus on how these robots can stay safe while learning new tasks in unpredictable environments.
